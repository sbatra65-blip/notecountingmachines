This week's biggest story comes from Ahmedabad, where police caught two couriers with 1,666 imitation ₹500 notes packed into bundles with fake bank labels, part of a scam on traders expecting large cash transfers. Similar "prop money" turned up this week in Thailand and Switzerland, and the lesson for any Indian shop is the same: a bundle with a neat label is not proof, so count and check every note.

## Country by country

### India

**Ahmedabad, Gujarat.** Ghatlodia police set a decoy trap after a complainant reported a ₹10 lakh cash transfer from Nagpur. On 19 November they arrested two men near a petrol pump with 17 bundles of 98 notes each, 1,666 notes that looked like ₹500 notes and carried fake ICICI Bank labels, IANS reported. The notes bore words such as "Five Hundred Coupon" and were not legal tender. Police say the same group had delivered eight bundles, said to hold ₹40 lakh, to a trader in Unjha on 17 November, which also turned out to be imitation notes. Three more suspects are being sought, and the case is registered under the BNS.

**Lakhimpur, Assam.** Bangalmara police raided a house in Lakhimpur district on 21 November and seized 40 bundles of suspected fake ₹500 notes, arresting the alleged supplier, The Sentinel reported. The notes have been sent for verification and police are tracing the wider network.

**How India is managing it:** local police raids and decoy operations, BNS cheating and forgery charges, and RBI rules that require banks to machine-check notes and impound fakes.

### Thailand

Immigration police arrested a 42-year-old foreign national in Samut Prakan and found 54 counterfeit US$100 notes (US$5,400, about 1,82,600 baht), Khaosod English reported on 19 November. He told officers the notes came from Cambodia. Separately, Pattaya Mail reported on 21 November that businesses in Phuket's Kata, Karon and Patong areas were warned after foreign visitors allegedly mixed imitation dollar notes in with real ones at money exchanges.

**Approach:** police checks on suspects' residences, and local warnings telling exchange booths not to complete a transaction until each note is verified.

### Switzerland

Customs officers at the Frauenfeld parcel centre seized fake notes worth about **€53,485 and US$14,894** in a parcel sent from Germany and declared as "fictitious money", the Swiss news agency ATS reported on 16 November. Because the notes matched real ones in size and look, customs treated them as counterfeit and passed the case to cantonal police.

**Approach:** strict law (any replica that can be confused with real money counts as counterfeit) and public warnings against buying "movie money" or "prop copy" notes online.

### Ghana

National Security officers arrested a church leader on 18 November over alleged conspiracy to counterfeit currency, GhanaWeb reported. Others linked to the case were picked up in June; he is held by the Bureau of National Investigations. This comes as Ghana prepares to issue its new Heritage Series cedi notes on 1 December.

**Approach:** national security agencies handle currency crime, alongside a full note redesign.

### Madagascar

Police in Toamasina seized fake notes worth **600 million ariary** on 6 November after a tip-off, L'Express de Madagascar reported. Police say fakes were being used to buy farm produce in rural areas ahead of the lychee season; a suspect is in custody and police are looking for others.

**Approach:** economic-crime police acting on tip-offs, with focus on rural markets where checks are weakest.

## What India can learn

- **Labels prove nothing.** The Gujarat bundles carried fake bank labels. Treat every bundle as loose notes until counted and checked.
- **Ban the look-alikes, not just the fakes.** Switzerland treats any confusable replica as counterfeit; in India, Gujarat police booked this week's "coupon" note case under BNS sections that cover notes resembling currency, cheating and forgery.
- **Warn the people who handle cash.** Phuket's alert went straight to exchange booths and shopkeepers, the people who see fakes first.
- **Rural markets need attention.** Madagascar's case shows fakes move where checking is weakest, like haats and mandis in India.

## What this means for your shop

- Never accept a large cash delivery by bundle count alone. Run every note through a counter with **UV, MG and IR** detection.
- Watch for "Five Hundred Coupon", "Children Bank" or "Movie Money" print; these show up fast when you look at each note, not the top one.
- Train staff to check the watermark, security thread and colour-shifting ₹500 numeral on any note the machine rejects. Our guide on [how to check a ₹500 note](/blog/how-to-check-500-rupee-note-security-features/) walks through each feature.
- If you suspect a fake, refuse it politely, note the details and report it. Never pass it on.
