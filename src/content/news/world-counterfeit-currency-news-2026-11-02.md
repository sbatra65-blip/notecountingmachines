This week's biggest story is from Hyderabad, where railway police seized fake notes with a face value of ₹60 lakh from a single passenger on a Mumbai-bound train. Notes moving in that volume are meant for markets, so every shop counter on the route is a possible target.

## Country by country

### India

**₹60 lakh on a train.** On 31 October, the Government Railway Police at Secunderabad, working with the RPF, found counterfeit notes worth ₹60 lakh on the Mumbai LTT Express, which runs on a Jaipur–Hyderabad–Mumbai route. A 19-year-old from Bikaner district, Rajasthan, was arrested and a second suspect is absconding, Telangana Today reported on 1 November.

**NIA chargesheet in a Nepal-linked case.** The NIA filed a chargesheet in a special court in Patna on 31 October against two men accused of circulating fake notes smuggled in from Nepal. The case began with a seizure of 281 fake notes (face value ₹50,150) at a market in Madhubani district, Bihar, on 2 August, according to PTI.

**Smaller, local networks.** An IANS report of 30 October says agencies are seeing more small groups of three or four people and solo operators, alongside the older cross-border syndicates. Officials quoted say these local fakes are mostly spent in crowded markets on purchases of around ₹1,000 or less, which is exactly where small shops sit.

**How India is managing it:** NIA cases for organised rackets, railway and border policing, and RBI rules that require banks to check notes by machine and impound every fake.

### Australia

The Reserve Bank of Australia detected **40,613 counterfeit notes** in the 12 months to 31 March 2026, and 95% were $50 or $100 notes, as reported by Inside Retail on 9 October. The rate reached 20 fakes per million notes in the March quarter, the highest since June 2018 and up from 5.3 per million in December 2024. Separately, the Australian Border Force made about 700 detections of fake cash at the border in the last financial year (ABC News, 3 September).

**Approach:** polymer notes with a clear window, public "check two features" advice from the RBA, and businesses adding UV (blue-light) checks at the till. Fakes are not reimbursed, so the loss falls on whoever accepts them.

### South Korea

Bank of Korea data reported on 12 October show that fake or altered notes handed in by investigators rose from 483 in 2024 to **9,716 in 2025**, with 50,000-won notes making up 97.7%. Much of the jump came from one large case. The Bank spent about 30 million won updating its counterfeit-detection app, while a lawmaker urged stronger action against fakes being advertised on social media.

### Italy (euro area)

In Grosseto, Tuscany, the Guardia di Finanza reported on 15 October that it seized **436 fake euro notes worth over €14,600** in one year. Most were €20 (258 notes) and €50 (145 notes), passed at supermarkets, bars, hotels and fuel stations. The same day, Carabinieri in Naples arrested a man after finding €21,800 in fake €100 notes at his home.

**Approach:** a specialist anti-counterfeiting police unit, checks on cash collected by security firms and banks.

### Zambia

On 31 October the Bank of Zambia told the public that a security thread sitting in a slightly different position on two genuine notes is "neither a defect nor, on its own, an indication that a banknote is counterfeit." It asked people to check several features together and promoted its free banknote app as a learning guide.

### United States

Police in La Crescent, Minnesota, warned residents on 29 October after fake bills turned up at two local businesses. Their advice: check the watermark, the security thread (which glows under UV), the colour-shifting numeral and, on the $100, the 3-D ribbon.

## What India can learn

- **Track the numbers publicly.** Australia publishes counterfeit rates every six months, so businesses know when to tighten checks. Regular, easy-to-read updates help shops act early.
- **No single feature proves a note.** Zambia's message, check several features together, applies equally to the ₹500.
- **Mid-value notes lead everywhere.** Australia's $50/$100, Italy's €20/€50 and Korea's 50,000 won mirror India's ₹500 problem.
- **Local networks need local checks.** If fakes are now spent in small amounts at busy markets, the shop counter is the first line of defence.
