This week's biggest story is Ghana's new set of cedi banknotes, unveiled on 12 November with upgraded security features, while India marked ten years since demonetisation with a mixed record on fakes. For an Indian shop the lesson is the same from both: counterfeiters follow the most-used high-value note, and today in India that is the ₹500.

## Country by country

### India

**Ten years after demonetisation.** On 8 November, The News Minute set out the numbers a decade after the 2016 note ban. Counterfeits detected across all denominations fell from 70.1 per crore notes in 2015-16 to 13.4 in 2025-26 (RBI data cited by The Hindu). But fake ₹500 notes rose from 0.3 per crore in 2016-17 to 20.1 in 2025-26. Cash has not gone away either: currency with the public was 12.1% of GDP on 31 March 2026, slightly above the 11.9% of 2015-16, even as UPI now handles most payment transactions by volume.

**Shahjahanpur, Uttar Pradesh.** Sadar Bazar police, with the SOG and surveillance cell, arrested five people including a married couple on 13 November. They recovered fake ₹500 notes worth ₹1,01,500, Amar Ujala reported. Police say the group passed notes at crowded places such as fairs in Shahjahanpur, Bareilly and Hardoi; one suspect is still being sought.

**Muzaffarpur, Bihar.** Police arrested a man in Muzaffarpur district with counterfeit notes worth about ₹14,000 (28 fake ₹500 notes, according to Amar Ujala), of which about ₹6,000 had already been spent in local markets, PTI reported on 12 November. Police are checking possible links to Nepal and Bangladesh.

**How India is managing it:** police and SOG raids on local gangs, NIA cases for cross-border networks, and RBI rules that require banks to machine-check notes and impound fakes.

### Ghana

President John Mahama and the Bank of Ghana unveiled the **Heritage Series** cedi notes in Accra on 12 November. All eight denominations (GH₵1 to GH₵200) continue, with improved security features, more durable reinforced cotton paper and a smaller, easier-to-handle format; the GH₵200 note in particular is being made smaller. The notes enter circulation on **1 December 2026**, and the old notes stay legal tender alongside them, Africanews and Graphic Online reported. Graphic Online also reported that a new Currency Act will replace the 1964 law, with tougher treatment of counterfeiting.

**Approach:** a full redesign, a public education campaign on the new features, and stronger laws.

### Philippines (with the US Secret Service)

On 12 November the US Secret Service gave a certificate of appreciation to the head of the Philippines' National Bureau of Investigation for a March 2026 operation in Pasay City, The Chronicle reported. In that case the NBI seized more than **US$200 million** in fake US currency and counterfeit treasury bonds and arrested one suspect (GMA News, 13 March 2026).

**Approach:** cross-border cooperation. Fake US dollars are used in scams worldwide, so the Secret Service works with local police well outside the US.

### Samoa

The National Bank of Samoa warned on 5 November of a rise in counterfeit **SAT $50 and $100** notes being presented at its branches. It asked shops and the public to check every note's watermark, security thread, print quality and paper feel, whoever it comes from.

**Approach:** bank-led public warnings when fakes rise.

### Indonesia (Bali)

On 27 October, Bali police and Bank Indonesia shredded **3,731 counterfeit rupiah notes** worth Rp 341.5 million, mostly Rp 100,000 notes, under Indonesia's 2011 Currency Law. Police urged the public to use the "3D" check: look, feel and hold to the light.

**Approach:** public destruction of seized fakes, plus a simple check that everyone can remember.

## What India can learn

- **New designs need public education.** Ghana is pairing its new notes with a national campaign. India's ₹500 checks only work if shop staff know them.
- **One overall number can hide one risky note.** India's total fakes fell after 2016, but ₹500 fakes rose sharply. Shops should judge risk by denomination.
- **Simple rules stick.** Indonesia's "look, feel, hold to light" is easy to remember at a busy counter.
- **Fakes travel.** The Philippines and Bihar cases both point to cross-border networks, so detection at the counter is the last line of defence.

## What this means for your shop

- Pass every ₹500 and ₹200 note through a counter with **UV, MG and IR** detection, even during a rush.
- Take extra care at melas, weddings and crowded markets, the places this week's Shahjahanpur gang targeted.
- Train staff to check the watermark, colour-shifting numeral and security thread on any note the machine rejects. Our guide on [how to check a ₹500 note](/blog/how-to-check-500-rupee-note-security-features/) shows each feature.
- If you suspect a fake, refuse it politely and stay safe. Never pass it on.
